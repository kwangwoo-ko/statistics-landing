Reading an ANOVA result, step by step

Three groups, one F statistic, two degrees of freedom — and the sentence you can actually write in a paper.

Advertisement
Updated November 2026 · Beginner-friendly · Uses built-in sample data

ANOVA is what you use when a t-test is not enough — because you have three or more groups. This walkthrough runs one on the sample data and reads the output line by line.

Step 1 — Load the sample data

Open GraphZen, click "Try the sample data", and keep all three columns checked: Control, Treatment_A, Treatment_B (20 values each).

Step 2 — Choose the graph

Set Graph Type to Box Plot with data points shown. You will see the pattern that motivates the test: Treatment_A's box sits clearly higher, while Control and Treatment_B overlap heavily.

Step 3 — Run the test

Select One-way ANOVA as the statistical test and press Generate Visualization. On the sample data you should see, approximately:

OutputExpected valueMeaning
F statistic≈ 35.2The ratio of between-group variation to within-group variation. Large F = group means sit far apart relative to the noise inside groups.
df (between)2Number of groups − 1 (3 groups → 2).
df (within)57Total observations − number of groups (60 − 3).
p-value< 0.0001The group means would be very unlikely to differ this much by chance.

Step 4 — What the result actually says

The correct sentence is narrow: "At least two of the group means differ significantly." ANOVA does not tell you which ones. In our sample, the group means are Control ≈ 24.1, Treatment_A ≈ 26.8, Treatment_B ≈ 24.2 — the follow-up question is obviously "is it A versus the rest?"

To answer that you need post-hoc pairwise comparisons (Tukey HSD is the classic choice). GraphZen reports the overall ANOVA; for the pairwise step, compare the pairs you care about with an adjusted significance threshold (for three pairs, α = 0.05/3 ≈ 0.017 by Bonferroni as a simple default), or use dedicated software for full post-hoc tables. Running a plain Welch's t-test on Treatment_A vs Control gives p ≈ 2.4 × 10⁻⁸; Treatment_B vs Control gives p ≈ 0.79 — consistent with the box plot's story.

Why not just run three t-tests? Because each test has a 5% false-positive risk, and three tests compound it to roughly 14%. ANOVA controls the overall error rate first; that is why "ANOVA, then post-hoc" is the standard sequence.

Step 5 — Write it up

A complete, honest sentence for this result:

"A one-way ANOVA found a statistically significant difference between the three groups (F(2, 57) = 35.2, p < 0.0001). Post-hoc comparisons indicated that Treatment_A differed from Control."

Notice what is included: the statistic, both degrees of freedom, the p-value, and a clear statement of what the follow-up found. That is the full pattern — the same one to follow with your own data.

When the ANOVA is not significant

If p > your threshold, the honest conclusion is that the data does not provide evidence that any group means differ — not that all groups are identical. With small samples, absence of evidence is often just missing evidence; see statistical power.

If your data is skewed

ANOVA assumes roughly symmetric distributions. If the box plots show strong skew or outliers, use the rank-based equivalent — Kruskal-Wallis — which GraphZen also runs in the same dropdown, and report medians rather than means. The reasoning is in parametric vs non-parametric tests.
