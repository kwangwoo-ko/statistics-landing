What is a p-value, really?

The most-used and most-misread number in science. Here is the version you can actually remember.

Advertisement
Updated November 2026 · Plain English · No formulas required

Your t-test just returned p = 0.03. Does that mean there is a 3% chance your hypothesis is wrong? No. Does it mean there is a 97% chance it is right? Also no. Both readings are extremely common — and both are wrong.

The correct sentence

A p-value answers exactly one question:

If there were truly no difference (or no relationship), what is the probability of seeing data at least this extreme? That probability is the p-value.

So p = 0.03 means: if the null hypothesis were true, results this extreme would happen about 3 times in 100. It says nothing directly about the probability that the null hypothesis is true — that would require prior beliefs about how likely a real effect is, which is a different (Bayesian) question entirely.

Why 0.05?

There is nothing magical about 0.05. It is a convention — a shared line for "surprising enough to take seriously" — that became standard in the mid-20th century. It is not a law of nature, and crossing it does not change what your data is. A result at p = 0.049 and one at p = 0.051 are equally interesting; they simply fall on different sides of an arbitrary line.

That is why good papers report the actual p-value (p = 0.032) rather than just "significant". And why "p = 0.06, a trend toward significance" is a red flag — either the evidence is there or it is not; the number speaks for itself.

What a p-value does not tell you

MisreadingReality
"p = 0.03 → 3% chance the null hypothesis is true"It is the probability of the data given the null, not the probability of the null given the data.
"p < 0.05, so the effect is big and important"Statistical significance is not practical importance. With a large enough sample, tiny meaningless differences become "significant". Always look at effect size.
"p > 0.05, so there is no effect"Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Small samples often fail to detect real effects (low power).
"p = 0.001 is more 'true' than p = 0.04"A smaller p means the data would be rarer under the null — nothing more. Both are just evidence against the null.

What to report alongside it

A p-value alone is a thin description of a result. The scientific standard is to report the p-value together with:

  • the effect size — how big is the difference? (the group means or medians, the slope, etc.)
  • a confidence interval — how precise is the estimate?
  • the sample size — how much data is this conclusion built on?

One-sentence summary to remember: the p-value is the chance of seeing data this surprising if nothing were going on. Small p = surprising. It is not the chance you are wrong.
