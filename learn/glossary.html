Statistics glossary

The terms people keep half-remembering — each in one honest paragraph, with the mistake it usually comes with.

P-value

The probability of seeing data at least as extreme as yours if there were no effect. Smaller p means your data would be more surprising under "nothing is going on".

Common mistake: reading it as "the probability the hypothesis is wrong".

α (alpha), the significance level

The threshold you compare p-values against — conventionally 0.05. Set it before looking at the data, not after.

Common mistake: interpreting 0.05 as a law of nature rather than a convention.

Paired vs independent data

Independent: different individuals in each group. Paired: the same individual measured twice (before/after). Paired designs need paired tests — they are usually more powerful because each subject is its own control.

Common mistake: running an independent-groups test on paired data.

t-test (Student's)

Compares the means of two independent groups. It assumes roughly normal data and equal variances.

Common mistake: using it on skewed data or wildly unequal group sizes.

Welch's t-test

A t-test variant that does not assume equal variances. It is the safer default for two-group comparisons and loses almost nothing when variances happen to be equal.

Common mistake: assuming Student's version is the "standard" and Welch's is exotic — it is the other way around in practice.

One-way ANOVA

Compares the means of three or more independent groups. A significant ANOVA says "at least two groups differ" — it does not say which.

Common mistake: running multiple t-tests instead of ANOVA, which inflates the false-positive rate.

Mann-Whitney U test

The rank-based alternative to a t-test for two independent groups. Compares distributions rather than means, so it tolerates skew and outliers. Report medians, not means.

Common mistake: reporting "mean ± SD" in a paper that ran Mann-Whitney.

Kruskal-Wallis test

The rank-based alternative to one-way ANOVA for three or more groups. A significant result means at least two distributions differ; follow up with pairwise comparisons.

Common mistake: interpreting a significant H as proof that all groups differ.

Median & IQR

The median is the middle value. The IQR (interquartile range) is the span of the middle 50% of values — the width of the box in a box plot. Both are resistant to outliers.

Common mistake: calling the middle line of a box plot "the mean".

SD — standard deviation

The typical distance of individual values from their mean. Describes how spread out the data is. Does not shrink as the sample grows.

Common mistake: confusing it with the precision of the mean (see SEM).

SEM — standard error of the mean

SD ÷ √n. Describes how precisely the mean is estimated. Shrinks as the sample grows — which is why SEM bars look smaller than SD bars.

Common mistake: using SEM to imply the data is tightly clustered.

95% confidence interval

The range of values compatible with your estimate at the conventional 95% level. Widely preferred over p-values alone because it shows both the effect size and its precision.

Common mistake: saying "95% of the data lie inside the CI" — the CI is about the mean, not the data.

Normality

Whether the data is roughly bell-shaped. Check with a box plot or histogram first; formal normality tests are unreliable at small sample sizes.

Common mistake: declaring data normal purely because a test with n = 6 failed to reject it.

Statistical power

The chance of detecting a real effect of a given size. Power grows with sample size. A non-significant result from a small study often means "we could not tell", not "nothing is there".

Common mistake: treating "not significant" as proof of no effect.

R² — coefficient of determination

The fraction of the variation in Y explained by X in a regression — 0.7 means 70%. A measure of fit, not of importance or causation.

Common mistake: reading a high R² as evidence that X causes Y.
