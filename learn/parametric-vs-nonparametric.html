Parametric vs non-parametric tests

Means are wonderful — until a single extreme value moves them anywhere it likes. Here is when to switch to ranks.

Updated November 2026 · Plain English · Decision table included

Statistics has two families of comparison tests. Parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA) work with means and assume a roughly bell-shaped distribution. Non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis) work with ranks and make almost no assumptions about shape. Neither family is "better" — they answer slightly different questions under different conditions.

The comparison

ParametricNon-parametric
ExamplesStudent's t-test, Welch's t-test, one-way ANOVAMann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis
What it comparesMeansDistributions, via ranks (report medians)
AssumesRoughly normal data; t-test also assumes equal variance (Welch's does not)Basically nothing about shape
StrengthMore powerful when the assumptions holdRobust to skew and outliers; safer with tiny samples
WeaknessOne extreme value can drag a mean and flip a conclusionIgnores how far apart values are — only their order

How to choose, practically

  1. Plot the data. A box or violin plot takes ten seconds and settles most of the question. Symmetric box, median centred, modest whiskers → parametric. Long tail, median far from centre, points trailing off → non-parametric.
  2. Consider the sample size. With n ≈ 30+ per group the t-test is fairly robust even to moderate skew. With n = 5, a single outlier can break it — and formal normality tests are too weak to warn you.
  3. Prefer Welch's t-test for two groups. It drops the equal-variance assumption, matches Student's performance when variances are equal, and wins when they are not.
  4. Match the summary to the test. Used a rank-based test? Report medians and IQR. A paper that runs Mann-Whitney and reports "mean ± SD" is mixing two stories.

What "non-parametric" does not mean: it does not mean the data is "bad" or that you failed a normality check. Skewed data is normal in biology, economics and engineering — rank-based tests are simply the appropriate tool for it.

The outlier that flips a conclusion

Imagine group A = 10, 11, 12, 13, 40 and group B = 8, 9, 10, 11, 12. The single 40 moves A's mean from ~11.5 to ~17. A t-test may well call the groups different. A Mann-Whitney test sees one unusual rank out of ten and says: not much evidence. Both statements are true — they answer "are the means different?" versus "do the groups tend to be different?". Your research question decides which one you are asking.

Ties and small samples

Mann-Whitney's exact p-value is computable for small samples without ties; with ties (common in ratings and counts) statisticians use a tie-corrected normal approximation.
